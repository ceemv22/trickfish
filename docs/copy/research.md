# Copy research: sounding human, selling to chess players

Researched 2026-11-10 with web search. The search tool returned summaries and short quoted snippets. Direct page fetches were blocked in this environment (en.wikipedia.org and styleguide.mailchimp.com weren't on the session's allowed network), so anything in quotation marks below is only what a search result quoted. Where I couldn't confirm a primary source, I say so.

## 1. Tells that make copy read as AI-written

Wikipedia's editors and Pangram both make the same point: one tell proves nothing, but a cluster of them reads as AI to most people. We treat the list as a ban for our own copy anyway, because our readers have learned to spot it and we gain nothing from sounding like that.

### Checklist (run it on every line)

**Words and phrases**
- [ ] None of the vocabulary flagged by the Wikipedia guide and its write-ups: *delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, intricate, meticulous, underscore, landscape, realm, foster, robust, leverage, navigate, seamless, enhance, showcase, vibrant, boast, swift, emphasizing, highlighting*.
- [ ] No marketing filler: *elevate, unlock, unleash, supercharge, game-changer, next level, level up, take your X to the next level, master, dominate, crush, secret weapon, ultimate, comprehensive, powerful, revolutionary, cutting-edge*.
- [ ] No throat-clearing openers like *In today's fast-paced world, In an ever-evolving landscape, Whether you're X or Y, Let's dive in, Here's the thing*.
- [ ] No hedging stacks (*may, might, could potentially, it's important to note that, it's worth mentioning*). Say it or cut it.
- [ ] No *In conclusion*, *In summary*, *Overall*, or a closing paragraph that repeats what was just said.

**Sentence patterns**
- [ ] No negative parallelism (*It's not just X, it's Y*, *not only X but also Y*, *This isn't X. It's Y.*). Pangram counts *not just X but Y* at about 3x the human rate. The Search Engine Land study (reprinted by PRSA) found *not only... but also* was the one tic with a clear negative effect on engagement.
- [ ] No reflexive triads (*fast, simple, and powerful*). Pangram puts triads at about 4x human frequency. A list of three is fine when there really are three things, but never three adjectives in a row and never two triads in a row.
- [ ] Vary sentence length on purpose. When every sentence is the same length and shape, it reads like a model wrote it. Put a three-word sentence next to a twenty-word one.
- [ ] No rhetorical-question openers (*Ever wondered why...?*, *What if I told you...?*) and no *Ready to...?* closers. A question is allowed only when we really want the answer, and on X we do: "What do you play?"
- [ ] No grand claims or editorialising (*stands as a testament*, *plays a vital role*). The Wikipedia guide lists promotional tone and superficial commentary as tells.

**Punctuation and formatting**
- [ ] No em dashes. Pangram measured them at 17 per 10,000 words in AI text against 2 in human text. The engagement study found em dashes didn't hurt performance, but readers still name them first as an AI sign, so we use a period, colon or comma. En dashes in number ranges (1200–1599) are fine.
- [ ] No emoji bullets, no rocket, fire or brain emojis, no 🧵 thread emoji, no ✅. NCC Group calls emoji bullets a "straightforward" LLM sign. One deliberate emoji a month at most.
- [ ] Hashtags: zero. One only if a chess event is trending and we really belong in it.
- [ ] In posts, no Markdown-style bold and no bullet lists. Pangram has Markdown at about 12x and bullet lists at about 9x in AI text. On the Gumroad page, bullets are fine for the file list only.
- [ ] No Title Case Headlines In Running Copy. Use sentence case.

**Claims**
- [ ] No generic superlatives (*the best, the most effective, the deadliest*) unless a number from our data proves it, and then we give the number instead.
- [ ] No invented social proof, rating-gain promises, time-saving claims or "thousands of players" lines.

## 2. What good human copywriters do

- **Write for the reader.** Jason Fried (37signals): "Most copywriting on the web sucks because it's written for the writer, not for the reader. Write for the reader." He also complains about copy that goes through layers of review until it's "a bunch of clever sounding stuff that doesn't say anything."
- **Be specific.** Joanna Wiebe is credited, in secondary sources, with "Vague is the enemy of conversion". Copyblogger argues that concrete facts make claims believable, and that specifics which add no clarity do harm. Gary Bencivenga's rule, as quoted in one source: "Never make your claim bigger than your proof." For us that means 2,404 games, 31%, 7...Ke8, 82%: real numbers with their denominators.
- **Clear first, funny second.** Mailchimp's style guide describes a voice that's human, familiar, friendly and straightforward, and says its sense of humour comes after clarity. Its old "this but not that" list (fun but not childish, clever but not silly, expert but not bossy) is a good model for a voice page. Mailchimp also tells writers to use active voice, short sentences and plain words.
- **Have a character and an enemy.** Write-ups on Liquid Death describe a brand run "more like a character" than a guideline book, using dark humour aimed at conventions, never at people. Oatly's voice is described as strategy first: talk like a human, use contractions, be self-aware. The lesson is to pick a target. Ours is the engine-perfect prep that a 1400 opponent will never play into, and the gambit that loses when they find the answer. Never the opponent, and never the reader.
- **Show, don't claim.** Don't write "brutal trap". Write "6.Nc6+ wins the queen". The board and the number do the persuading.
- **Insider language.** Chess players read "hangs", "drops a pawn", "on the rim", "the h8 rook is next", "discovered check" and "book move" faster than any explanation. Annotation symbols (?, ??, !) are free punchiness. Use them the way a club player would.
- **Earned humour.** One dry line per post, tied to the position. A joke that would work about any product doesn't belong.
- **UX-writing rules** (general UX guidance; I found no published Revolut or Uber guides): clear, short, useful; the same word for the same thing every time (we always say "scored", never "won", for points per game).

## 3. Chess on X: what lands, what gets scrolled past

The sources here were thin: forum posts and marketing blogs, with no engagement data for chess posts on X. These points are our judgement, informed by them.

- **Puzzles are what people engage with.** A chess YouTube guide says puzzle content beats most other formats and recommends the "pause here and find the move" prompt. Our morning post does that.
- **Players hate being sold to mid-game.** A Chess.com forum thread about an ad showing up after one move ends with the poster switching to Lichess. Ads interrupt, and our question posts mustn't feel like one. No link, no price, no "pack" in the morning.
- **Creators who win have a voice.** Write-ups on GothamChess credit humour, blunt honesty about bad moves, and storytelling backed by real credentials. Ours is the data: we can be blunt because we have 902,003 games behind us.
- **Lichess's own account** mixes results, streams and playful posts, and leans on the fact that it's free and has no ads. Our Lichess-database origin (CC0) is a point of credibility with that crowd. Say it plainly.
- **What reads as an ad:** "Master the Italian in 7 days!", rating-gain promises, "secret", "every GM uses", countdown timers, emoji-led threads, links in the first post.

## 4. Platform specifics

### X
- **Limits:** bio 160 characters, display name 50, location 30. The X API docs confirm name and location. 160 for the bio comes from third-party guides but is consistent. The website field doesn't count toward the bio.
- **Characters:** a standard post is 280 characters and shows in full. X counts a link as 23 characters. Avoid the single-character ellipsis (…), which counts double; write 3...g6 with three periods.
- **The first line decides.** Long (Premium) posts fold behind "Show more" after roughly 280 characters (some sources say 125–140 in the feed). Either way the hook has to be in the first line. We keep posts under 280, so nothing folds, and the first line still has to earn the second.
- **Links:** Buffer's analysis of 18.8M posts found link posts lost most of their reach after March 2025, especially for non-Premium accounts. Musk later said throttling had stopped, and X tested a new way to show links in late 2025. The evidence conflicts, so we play safe: the question post has no link, and the link goes in the evening answer reply. We never use "link in bio" bait.

### Gumroad
- The product page is the landing page. Remove distractions and keep the price near the button.
- **Summary:** the one line beside the buy button. It should say what you get, not how you'll feel.
- **Additional details:** short key/value rows. Good for format, page count, rating band and engine.
- **CTA:** choose from Gumroad's presets ("I want this!", "Buy this", "Pay") or use custom text where the editor allows. I couldn't confirm the preset list from Gumroad's docs.
- **Refunds:** Gumroad has moved toward one account-level refund policy (a 30-day money-back guarantee or no refunds). Some accounts still have per-product policies. Either way, a clear refund line takes the risk out of a $14 impulse buy, and a no-refund policy doesn't stop chargebacks anyway.
- **Description structure** (from the Gumroad-specific guides): headline, what it is, proof, what's inside, who it's for, objections, refund line, button. Screenshots of real pages beat adjectives.
